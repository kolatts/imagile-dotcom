I can usually tell within one screen share whether someone is fluent with an AI coding agent, and it has never once been about which tool they have open.

It's whether they send a second message.

## Course Completion Measures Course Completion

Most organizations rolling out AI tooling are tracking exactly the wrong thing, and I mean that specifically, not as a general complaint about metrics. Licenses issued, seats activated, training modules completed — every one of those measures whether someone was exposed to the tool. None of them measures whether the exposure changed how they work.

We've already made the case that [conventional training programs go obsolete](/blog/ai-training-costs-not-worth-it) before the cohort finishes them — the tools move faster than the curriculum. Set that argument aside for a moment, because it's not the interesting problem anymore. The interesting problem is what you'd actually measure even if the training stayed current: what does capability look like, concretely, in a way you could watch for on a screen share?

## The Second Message Is the Cheapest Upgrade You Can Buy

Four behaviors separate someone who uses an AI agent from someone who is fluent in it: delegation, description, discernment, and diligence. Delegation is deciding what to hand off and what to keep. Description is communicating the ask clearly enough that the first attempt has a real chance. Discernment is evaluating what comes back instead of accepting it. Diligence is staying accountable for what you ultimately ship, regardless of who — or what — wrote it.

Of the four, iteration is the strongest predictor of everything else, and the data backs this up with real numbers, not intuition. Conversations that iterate — where the person pushes back, adds a missing constraint, asks a clarifying question — correlate with clarifying goals up front at +23.6 percentage points and with providing concrete examples at +22.6 points, compared to conversations that accept the first output. The single cheapest behavior change available to almost anyone is sending the second message instead of stopping at the first.

## Polish Suppresses Checking, Which Is Exactly Backwards

Here's the finding that should unsettle anyone rolling out these tools at scale, because it runs directly against intuition. When output looks polished — clean formatting, confident tone, plausible citations — people check it *less*, not more. Fact-checking drops by 3.7 percentage points. Identifying missing context drops by 5.2 points. The better the output looks, the less anyone verifies it actually holds up.

Polish was never a correctness signal. It's a formatting signal, and treating the two as the same thing is how confidently wrong output ships past everyone who read it and nodded. This is discernment's entire job, and it's the behavior that erodes fastest exactly when it matters most — when the output looks too good to question.

## Accountability Is Shared Until the Moment It's Needed

Diligence is the least glamorous of the four and the one most often skipped in a training curriculum, because it doesn't show up in a demo. It's the habit of staying accountable for output regardless of what generated it — not treating "the AI wrote it" as a reason the mistake belongs to someone else.

Every org that's had a real incident involving AI-assisted work has discovered the same thing in the postmortem: accountability was assumed to be shared right up until it was needed, at which point it turned out to belong to nobody in particular. That's not a technology failure. It's a training gap, and it's the one gap that a feature announcement or a licenses-purchased count will never surface.

## You Can Observe All Four in Fifteen Minutes

None of this requires a certification exam to assess. Sit with someone for one real task and watch what they do. Do they hand off the whole thing or the right slice of it? Do they describe the goal or just paste a vague ask and hope? Do they read the output critically or skim it for something that looks plausible and move on? When it's wrong, do they own the miss or point at the tool?

That's a fifteen-minute observation, and it tells you more about an org's actual AI capability than a dashboard of course-completion percentages ever will. ## Fluency Is Observable. Completion Is Just a Timestamp.

The uncomfortable version of this argument, stated plainly: the person who taught your team is the worst possible judge of whether the lesson took. They're invested in the training looking successful. What you actually need is a way to observe the four behaviors directly, independent of who ran the workshop — because a completion certificate tells you someone was in the room, and that was never the question that mattered.
