Every developer I talk to about coding agents has already picked a side in a fight that happened on the railroads a hundred and fifty years ago. Most of them don't know it. One side is a folk hero with a hammer, and the other is a coal miner's kid who couldn't read, and the kid is the one you want to be.

## John Henry won the contest and failed the con save

Picture the heading of a railroad tunnel in the 1870s. The light is candles and grease lamps, and the air is rock dust and powder smoke with nowhere to go. A steel driver stands at the face swinging a hammer at a steel rod while his partner turns it a quarter turn between blows so the bit cuts fresh rock.

The ballad puts John Henry at the Big Bend Tunnel on the Chesapeake and Ohio line in West Virginia. The captain brings a steam drill to the face and, in the tradition of every manager who has ever seen a demo, frames it as a contest. John Henry tells him he'll die with a hammer in his hand before he lets that drill beat him down, and every version of the song collected since agrees that he did both.

The people who went looking for the man behind the song found three different deaths.

A witness named Spencer, interviewed in the 1920s, said the contest ran all day and that near the end John Henry fainted at the face, came to, said he was blind and dying, and asked for his wife. She held his head in her lap while the crew measured. Spencer put it at twenty-seven and a half feet to the drill's twenty-one, and you can [read the account](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Henry_%28folklore%29) on the marker in Talcott today.

Neal Miller, a water boy at Big Bend, told the folklorist Guy Johnson the song had it wrong. John Henry won the race, went back to work, and died months later when a blast brought the roof down. The bosses buried him at night under the fill at the east end of the tunnel so the crews wouldn't hear about it. Bernard Asbell laid both accounts out in [American Heritage in 1963](https://www.americanheritage.com/man-aint-nothin-man).

The third death is the one Scott Reynolds Nelson found in Virginia penitentiary records for [Steel Drivin' Man](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/steel-drivin-man-9780195341195). A nineteen-year-old convict named John William Henry was leased to the railroad, where prisoners drilled the Lewis Tunnel beside steam drills that broke down constantly and lost to the men beside them. The dust those drills threw into the air killed more than a hundred of those men, and the records for John Henry stop in 1873.

Whether the hammer took his heart, the rock took his body, or the dust took his lungs, John Henry stayed at the face and the tunnel got finished with drills.

## The senior developer is holding the hammer

I watch this exact story play out every week. A senior engineer sits down with a coding agent, hands it a task they could do in their sleep, watches it stumble, and concludes they're faster. They are. Steam drills in 1870 jammed and snapped bits, and coding agents two years ago wrote code a good developer could outpace without trying.

So they win the race, and then they stop. They spend the next eighteen months winning a contest against a tool that gets better every quarter while their own skill at using it stays at level one. The drill can't be bargained with, it can't be reasoned with, and it absolutely will not stop, and none of that changes because you beat it on a Tuesday.

The real John Henry was a convict with no choice about the tunnel, and the refusal in the ballad belongs to the men who sang it. The song is what people who loved a craft made out of watching a machine take it. If you're a developer feeling that right now, the song is for you, and so is the rest of this post.

## The colliery boy was grinding XP on a pumping engine

Go back fifty years and across an ocean to a cottage in Wylam, on the Tyne, where a horse-drawn coal wagonway ran past the front door. George Stephenson was born there in 1781, the son of a man who fed the fire of the colliery pumping engine for twelve shillings a week. None of the six kids went to school.

He herded cows, picked stones out of coal, and drove the horse that wound coal up the shaft. In the evenings, according to [Samuel Smiles's biography](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/46229/46229-h/46229-h.htm), he built model engines out of clay with hemlock stalks for pipes. By his late teens he was tending a real one and spending his own hours taking it to pieces and putting it back together to learn what every part did.

We have a word now for someone who starts using a powerful tool without the credentials to explain it, gets results, and can't yet tell you why. We call them a vibe coder, and it isn't a compliment, in roughly the way "multiclass bard" isn't a compliment. Stephenson was a vibe coder with no schooling and no theory, and the thing that separates him from the hobbyist next to him is that he never stopped taking the engine apart.

He could not read a word of the engineering that existed, so at eighteen he paid for night school out of his wages. He was a brakesman at Killingworth colliery, mending shoes and clocks on the side, when the pumping engine at the High Pit failed. It had been failing for most of a year, every engineer in the district had tried and given up, and the pit had turned into a water level, which every gamer knows is the worst level.

Stephenson had been walking over to look at it on his own time. He told the head viewer he could alter her and make her draw, and have the men back at the bottom of the pit within a week. He got the job because nothing else had worked, picked his own crew, stripped the engine, changed what he'd worked out was wrong, and had the pit dry in a few days.

They made him engine-wright, responsible for every engine at the colliery. Three years later he built his first locomotive and named it Blücher after a Prussian field marshal, which remains the most metal thing anyone has ever named a train. The rails, the gauge most of the world still runs on, the Stockton and Darlington, and the Rocket at Rainhill all came after, and every one of them was built by a man who learned engines by breaking them on purpose.

## Taking the tool apart is the whole skill

The developers who come out the other side of this as better engineers do it the Stephenson way. They read the output instead of accepting it. They work out why the agent chose the shape it did, notice what it needs to be told and what it fabricates when it hasn't been told, and build the rails and gauge around it: the tests, the context, the review step.

Stephenson wasn't smarter than the engineers who couldn't fix the High Pit engine. He was the one who'd spent his evenings inside the machine, and he was willing to say he could fix it before he was sure. The [Science Museum's biography](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/people/cp3870/george-stephenson) calls him a self-taught doctor of engines, which is what a vibe coder becomes if they don't quit.

If you're new to this and using the tools on vibes, good. Take the output apart, ask why it built the thing that way, and check whether it's right. Learn to read the code it gives you the way Stephenson learned to read.

If you've been doing this for twenty years and you're winning the race, put the hammer down for a month. Give the agent work you'd give a strong junior, review it the way you'd review theirs, and write down what it needed from you to do it well. That list is your first set of rails.
