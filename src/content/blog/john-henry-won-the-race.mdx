The last time a machine showed up to do skilled work faster than the people already doing it, the railways were being built. Two names from that era keep coming back to me when I sit with a team that's deciding what to do about AI. One of them is a legend. The other kept a notebook.

## The man who raced it

You know the song even if you don't think you do. John Henry was a steel-driving man. His job was to swing a hammer at a steel rod, over and over, so a partner could turn it between blows and a hole would slowly open in solid rock. Pack the hole with black powder, light it, clear the rubble, start again. That is how you cut a tunnel through a mountain in 1870.

The ballad puts him at the Big Bend Tunnel on the Chesapeake and Ohio line in West Virginia. The captain announces he's bringing a steam drill around. John Henry answers that before he lets that steam drill beat him down, he'll die with his hammer in his hand. They race. He drives fourteen feet to the drill's nine. Then he lies down and dies, hammer still in his grip.

The historian Scott Reynolds Nelson spent years trying to find the real man, and made a good case that he was a convict laborer leased out of the Virginia Penitentiary, working a different tunnel on the same line. The detail I can't shake from that research is about the drills. The early steam drills were terrible. They jammed, they broke bits, they choked on rock dust, and crews spent as much time fixing them as drilling. A strong team of men really could outwork one on a given day.

Which means the ballad isn't exaggerating. John Henry probably could win. That's what makes it a tragedy rather than a tall tale. He won the race, and the tunnel still got finished with drills.

## The man who never raced it

George Stephenson is the other story, and it's a quieter one. He was born in 1781 in a mining village in the north of England, the son of a man who tended a pumping engine. He couldn't read until he paid for night classes at eighteen. He worked as a brakesman at a colliery, tending the winding gear, and he got noticed the way engineers still get noticed: a pumping engine at the pit had beaten everyone who tried to fix it, and he fixed it.

He didn't invent the locomotive. Richard Trevithick ran one a decade before Stephenson built his first, and it kept breaking the cast-iron rails under it. What Stephenson did was treat the engine as the least interesting part of the problem. His first locomotive, the Blücher of 1814, hauled thirty tons up a grade at about four miles an hour, which sounds unimpressive until you realize it was the first to run flanged wheels on plain rails and actually keep going. Then he worked on the rails themselves, moving to wrought iron. Then on the gauge, settling on the four feet, eight and a half inches that most of the world's track still uses. Then on gradients, because he'd figured out early that a locomotive loses a large share of its pulling power on even a gentle slope, so the line had to be nearly flat, which meant surveying, cutting, and embanking on a scale nobody had attempted.

In 1825 he stood before a Parliamentary committee to defend the Liverpool and Manchester Railway and got taken apart by a lawyer over his rough survey figures and his Northumbrian accent. The bill failed. He came back the following year with a better survey, got it through, and then floated the line across Chat Moss, a peat bog everyone had told him was impassable, on a bed of brushwood and heather.

In 1829 the railway's directors still weren't sure locomotives were the right answer and held an open competition at Rainhill. The Rocket, built by his son Robert's firm with a multi-tube boiler suggested by the railway's own treasurer, was the only engine to finish the trials without breaking down. It reached around thirty miles an hour. The line opened the next year.

Notice the shape of that career. The engine was a small part of it. Most of the work was everything around the engine that had to change before the engine was worth having.

## The two postures

I bring these two up because I keep watching people and teams pick a posture toward AI, and the postures have names.

The John Henry posture is the race. A senior developer sits down with a coding assistant, gives it a task they could do in their sleep, watches it stumble, and concludes they're faster. They are. That's the point of the story. The steam drills of 1870 were bad, and the coding tools of two or three years ago were bad in the same way. Anyone who tried one then and walked away won a real race against a real machine.

But look at where the race gets you. It proves something about today. It says nothing about the tunnel. And it costs you the thing John Henry paid with, which in our case isn't a life but is something you don't get back: the months where the tool was getting better and you weren't learning how to work with it.

The Stephenson posture is harder to describe because it doesn't have a dramatic moment. It looks like this: assume the engine will improve on its own schedule, and spend your effort on what has to be true around it. What does it need to know before it starts? What's the shape of a task it can carry, and what's the shape of one it can't? Where does the work get checked, by whom, and how do you know the check happened? What's the equivalent of the rails, the gauge, and the flat grade for the way your team actually ships?

None of that is glamorous. Stephenson's most consequential decision might have been a number of inches. But it's the part that compounds, because every improvement to the engine lands on rails you already laid.

## Who set up the race

There's a character in the ballad who doesn't get enough attention: the captain. He's the one who brought the drill around and framed it as a contest. John Henry's pride did the rest.

If you lead a team, that's the role you're at risk of playing. "Let's see if the AI can do it faster" is a race. So is a quarterly target for percent of code written by a tool, or a quiet expectation that headcount will follow the demo. People will respond to a race the way John Henry did, by proving they can win it, and some of them will. You'll have spent your budget on a contest that tells you nothing about how to build the railway.

The alternative is to stop asking who's faster and start asking what has to change around the tool for it to carry real weight. That question is boring, and it's the whole job.

## What Stephenson didn't get right

I don't want to leave him a saint. On the opening day of the Liverpool and Manchester Railway, a member of Parliament named William Huskisson stepped onto the track to shake hands with the Duke of Wellington and was struck by the Rocket. He died that evening. The system Stephenson built had a gap in it where a person was standing, and nobody had thought hard enough about what happens when a fast machine meets someone who has never seen one.

That's a fair warning for the Stephenson posture too. Building around the engine includes building the parts that protect the people near it. Every team I've seen adopt these tools well spent real time on where the human stands and what stops the train.

## The hammer

The last verse of the ballad has a line that the men who kept singing it after John Henry died added themselves: this old hammer killed John Henry, but it won't kill me. That's the correct lesson, and it's the one I'd offer anyone who's been quietly racing the machine and winning. You're allowed to be proud of the fourteen feet. Then put the hammer down and go look at the rails.
