Two men met the same machine about fifty years apart, on opposite sides of the Atlantic. One raced it. One took it apart. Every conversation I have about AI with a developer or a team ends up being about which of those two they've decided to be, whether they know it or not.

## The hammer comes down for the last time

Picture the heading of a railroad tunnel in the 1870s. The light is candles and grease lamps. The air is rock dust and powder smoke that has nowhere to go. A steel driver stands at the face swinging a hammer at a steel rod while a partner, the shaker, turns it a quarter turn between blows so the bit cuts fresh rock.

The ballad puts John Henry at the Big Bend Tunnel on the Chesapeake and Ohio line near Talcott, West Virginia. The captain brings a steam drill to the face and John Henry tells him that before he lets that drill beat him down, he'll die with a hammer in his hand. The versions collected over the last century agree on what happened next: he beat the drill, and then he drove so hard he broke his poor heart, laid down his hammer, and died.

The people who went looking for the man behind the song found three different deaths.

The first came from a witness named Spencer, interviewed in the 1920s, whose account is on the [historical marker in Talcott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Henry_%28folklore%29) today. He said the contest ran all day. Near the end John Henry fainted at the face, came to, said he was blind and dying, and asked for his wife. She held his head in her lap while the crew measured, and he asked her whether he had beaten that old steam drill. He had. Spencer put it at twenty-seven and a half feet to the drill's twenty-one.

The second came from Neal Miller, a water boy at Big Bend, who told the folklorist Guy Johnson the song had it wrong. John Henry won the race and went back to work. He died months later when a blast brought rock down in the heading, and the bosses, afraid of trouble among the crews, put him in a box with another man and buried him at night under the fill at the east end of the tunnel. Bernard Asbell laid out both accounts in [American Heritage in 1963](https://www.americanheritage.com/man-aint-nothin-man).

The third is the one the historian Scott Reynolds Nelson found in Virginia penitentiary records for [Steel Drivin' Man](https://global.oup.com/academic/product/steel-drivin-man-9780195341195). A nineteen-year-old from New Jersey named John William Henry was convicted of theft in 1866 and leased out to the railroad, where convicts drilled the Lewis Tunnel day and night beside steam drills. The drills were powerful and unreliable, and the convicts did outdrill them. Then the dust the drills threw into the air killed the men breathing it, more than a hundred of them, and the records for John Henry stop in 1873.

Three deaths, one shape. Whether the hammer took his heart, the rock took his body, or the dust took his lungs, John Henry stayed at the face and the tunnel got finished with drills.

## The colliery boy took the engine to pieces

Now go back fifty years and across an ocean to a cottage in Wylam, on the Tyne, where a horse-drawn coal wagonway ran past the front door. George Stephenson was born there in 1781, the second son of a man who fed the fire of the colliery pumping engine for twelve shillings a week. None of the six children went to school.

He herded cows at twopence a day. He picked stones out of coal, drove the gin horse that wound coal up the shaft, then became his father's assistant at the fire. In the evenings, according to [Samuel Smiles's biography](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/46229/46229-h/46229-h.htm), he built model engines out of clay with hemlock stalks for pipes.

By his late teens he was tending an engine himself, and he was spending his own hours taking it to pieces and putting it back together to learn what every part did. He could not read a word of the engineering that existed. So at eighteen he paid for night school out of his wages, and by nineteen he could sign his name.

He was a brakesman at Killingworth colliery, mending shoes and clocks on the side to feed his family, when the pumping engine at the High Pit failed. It had been failing for the better part of a year. Every engineer in the district had been called in and none of them could make it draw, and the pit was drowning.

Stephenson had been going over to look at it on his own time. He told the head viewer he could alter her and make her draw, and have the men back at the bottom of the pit within a week. He got the job because nothing else had worked. He picked his own crew, stripped the engine, changed what he'd worked out was wrong, and had the pit dry in a few days.

They made him engine-wright, responsible for every engine at the colliery. Three years later he built the Blücher, his first locomotive. Then came the safety lamp, the wrought-iron rails, the gauge that most of the world still runs on, the Stockton and Darlington, the Liverpool and Manchester, and the Rocket at Rainhill.

## The vibe coder is a brakesman with a broken engine

We have a word for a person who starts using a powerful tool without the credentials to explain it, gets results, and can't yet tell you why. We call them a vibe coder, and it isn't a compliment.

Stephenson was a vibe coder. He had no schooling and no theory. He had a machine in front of him that worked or didn't, and the willingness to take it apart on his own time until he knew why.

That last part is the whole story. The tinkering by itself is where most people stop, and it produces a hobbyist. Stephenson kept pulling the engine apart until he could predict it, taught himself to read so he could learn what other people already knew, and then walked into a problem the credentialed engineers had given up on. The [Science Museum's short biography](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/people/cp3870/george-stephenson) calls him a self-taught doctor of engines, which is what a vibe coder becomes if they don't quit.

I watch developers do this with coding agents right now. The ones who come out the other side as better engineers do it the Stephenson way. They read the output instead of accepting it, work out why the agent chose the shape it did, notice what it needs to be told and what it fabricates when it hasn't been told, and build the rails and gauge around it: the tests, the context, the review step. The tool doesn't make them an engineer. Taking it apart does.

## The steel driver refused the new tool, and it made no difference

The John Henry of the ballad is a master of his craft who meets the machine that will replace the craft and decides to beat it instead of learning it. The uncomfortable part of the story is that he can. Steam drills in 1870 broke down constantly, and coding agents two years ago wrote code that a good senior developer could outpace without trying. Anyone who tried one then and walked away won a real race.

That posture is everywhere right now. A senior developer hands the agent a task they could do in their sleep, watches it stumble, and concludes they're faster. They are. Then they stop, and they spend the next eighteen months winning a contest against a tool that gets better every quarter while their own skill at using it stays at zero.

None of the three accounts of John Henry's death change the ending, because in every one of them he stayed at the face. Winning didn't buy him anything except the song.

I'll say the plain thing about the real John Henry, too. He was a convict with no choice about the tunnel, and the refusal in the ballad belongs to the men who sang it, not to him. The song is what people who loved a craft made out of watching a machine take it. If you're a developer feeling that right now, that's the feeling the song is for.

## Which one you are is a decision, not a talent

Stephenson wasn't smarter than the engineers who couldn't fix the High Pit engine. He was the one who'd spent his evenings inside the machine, and he was willing to say he could fix it before he was sure.

If you're new to this and using the tools on vibes, good. Take the output apart. Ask why it built the thing that way and check whether it's right. Learn to read the code it gives you the way Stephenson learned to read, because that's what turns the tinkering into engineering.

If you've been doing this for twenty years and you're winning the race, put the hammer down for a month. Give the agent work you'd give a strong junior, review it the way you'd review theirs, and pay attention to what it needs from you to do it well. The tunnel is getting finished with drills either way. The only open question is whether you're the one who knows how they work.
