I spent years in a metal band writing songs about blue-collar men in Rust Belt towns, so I'm predisposed to like John Henry. He's the wrong model for what to do about AI anyway. The right one is a Northumbrian engineer most people have never heard of.

## The steel-driving man could actually win

John Henry drove steel: he swung a hammer at a rod while a partner turned it, opening a hole in rock for black powder. The ballad puts him at the Big Bend Tunnel on the Chesapeake and Ohio line in West Virginia, around 1870. The captain brings a steam drill around, John Henry says he'll die with his hammer in his hand before it beats him down, and they race.

He drives fourteen feet to the drill's nine. Then he lies down and dies.

The historian Scott Reynolds Nelson spent years looking for the real man and made a strong case that he was a convict laborer leased out of the Virginia Penitentiary. The detail that stuck with me was about the drills. The early steam drills jammed, snapped bits, and choked on dust, so a strong crew really could outwork one on a given day.

That's what makes it a tragedy instead of a tall tale. He won, and the tunnel still got finished with drills.

## Stephenson treated the engine as the boring part

George Stephenson was born in 1781 in a mining village, the son of a man who tended a pumping engine. He paid for night school at eighteen to learn to read. He got noticed the way engineers still get noticed: a pumping engine at Killingworth colliery had beaten everyone sent to fix it, and he fixed it.

He did not invent the locomotive. Richard Trevithick ran one a decade earlier, and it kept breaking the cast-iron rails under it. Stephenson's first engine, the Blücher of 1814, hauled thirty tons at four miles an hour, and its real achievement was running flanged wheels on plain rails without wrecking them.

Then he spent his career on everything around the engine. Wrought-iron rails. A gauge of four feet, eight and a half inches that most of the world still runs on. Nearly flat gradients, because he'd measured how much pulling power a locomotive loses on even a gentle slope, which meant cuttings and embankments on a scale nobody had attempted.

In 1825 a Parliamentary committee took him apart over his rough survey figures and his accent, and the Liverpool and Manchester bill failed. He came back a year later with a better survey and won. Then he floated the line across Chat Moss, a peat bog everyone said was impassable, on a bed of brushwood and heather.

At the Rainhill trials in 1829, the Rocket was the only engine to finish without breaking down. The line opened the next year.

## Racing the tool tells you about today, not the tunnel

I keep watching people pick one of these two postures toward AI without knowing they're doing it.

The John Henry posture is the race. A senior developer hands a coding assistant a task they could do in their sleep, watches it stumble, and concludes they're faster. They are. The drills of 1870 were bad and the coding tools of three years ago were bad in the same way, and anyone who walked away then won a real race.

Look at where it gets you. It proves something about today and says nothing about the tunnel. And it costs the months where the tool was getting better and you weren't learning how to work with it.

The Stephenson posture has no dramatic moment. Assume the engine improves on its own schedule, and put your effort into what has to be true around it: what it needs to know before it starts, the shape of a task it can carry, where the work gets checked and by whom. That's the rails, the gauge, and the flat grade for how your team ships.

It's dull work, and it compounds. Every improvement to the engine lands on rails you already laid.

## The captain set up the race

The character nobody talks about in the ballad is the captain. He brought the drill around and framed it as a contest, and John Henry's pride did the rest.

If you lead a team, that's the role you're at risk of playing. "Let's see if the AI can do it faster" is a race. So is a target for percent of code written by a tool, or a quiet expectation that headcount follows the demo. People will answer a race by winning it, and some of them will, and you'll have learned nothing about how to build the railway.

## Stephenson's gap was where a person stood

On opening day in 1830, a Member of Parliament named William Huskisson stepped onto the track to shake hands with the Duke of Wellington and was struck by the Rocket. He died that evening. Nobody had thought hard enough about what happens when a fast machine meets someone who has never seen one.

Building around the engine includes building the parts that protect the people near it. Every team I've seen adopt these tools well spent real time on where the human stands and what stops the train.

The men who kept singing about John Henry after he died added a line of their own: this old hammer killed John Henry, but it won't kill me. Be proud of the fourteen feet. Then put the hammer down and go look at the rails.
